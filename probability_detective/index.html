Your briefing

Detective, we have three cases about chance

People say things like “this coin is hot, heads must be next” or “a 7 is lucky”. Your job is to check those claims with experiments — not with feelings. You will predict first, then collect real data, then explain what the data actually shows.

Time: about 10–15 minutes. You need: nothing but this page. No account, no name, no email, no money, no betting — and nothing you type leaves this device.

Case 1 · Coin Lab

Predict how often a coin lands heads, then run 10, 100 and 1,000 flips. Includes a clearly labelled biased coin whose chance you control.

Case 2 · Dice Lab

Roll one die, then compare the sums of two dice. Find out why some sums show up far more often than others.

Case 3 · Fair Game

Build a points game with exact rules, calculate the expected advantage, simulate it — and then design a brand-new fair game of your own.

What you will be able to do afterwards

  • Tell the difference between theoretical probability (what the maths says) and observed frequency (what actually happened in your run).
  • Explain why a small sample can look wildly different from the theory, using your own numbers.
  • Compare experiments at 10, 100 and 1,000 trials and describe what changes.
  • Explain what makes a simple game fair — using probabilities and expected points, not luck.

Two ideas behind everything above

Theoretical probability is a property of the coin: for a fair coin it is exactly 0.5. It does not change because of what happened in the last few flips. After ten heads in a row, the chance of heads on the next flip is still 0.5 — the coin does not remember, and it does not owe you a tails.

Observed frequency is what your run produced: heads divided by flips. With 10 flips it can easily be 0.7 or 0.3. With 1,000 flips it usually sits closer to the theoretical value — but that is a tendency, not a promise. A longer run can be further from 0.5 than a shorter one. What bigger samples do is make large misses much rarer.

Because the flips are independent, the running proportion is not “due” to correct itself. If it drifts back toward 0.5, that is the growing pile of ordinary flips diluting the early lucky streak — not the coin paying a debt.

=Are the sums equally likely? Here is the arithmetic

A sum is not one outcome — it is a whole family of outcomes. Two fair dice have 6 × 6 = 36 equally likely pairs, and different sums are made by different numbers of pairs:

ways(7) = 6 of 36 → 6 ÷ 36 = 0.1667
ways(2) = 1 of 36 → 1 ÷ 36 = 0.0278

Six ways against one way: a 7 is six times as likely as a 2. So the eleven sums are not equally likely, even though the dice themselves are perfectly fair. Fairness of the parts does not mean fairness of the totals.

Case 3

The Fair-Game Designer

Two players roll one die each turn. Each of them wins points only when their own condition comes up, and their conditions never overlap. Your job: work out whether the two sides get the same expected advantage per turn — and if not, fix it with arithmetic instead of hope.

□

Points here are counters on this page. They are not money, not tokens, not a score you can lose, and nothing is recorded about you. This is an arithmetic model of fairness, and it is the same arithmetic you would use to check a real claim about a game.

The rules, written out exactly
  1. One fair six-sided die is rolled once per turn. Each face (1–6) has probability 1/6.
  2. Player You win your points if the roll matches your condition. The other player (Rival, just a name for the other side of the calculation) wins their points if the roll matches theirs.
  3. Most rolls match nobody, and then nobody scores. A turn can also be a draw, but never a double win.
  4. The die is fair and independent: the previous roll never changes the next one.
  5. Expected points per turn for a side = (probability that their condition comes up) × (points they receive). That number is the average over a very long series of turns.

Why expected points decide fairness

A single turn tells you almost nothing: on one roll the die either matches your condition or it does not. Fairness is a statement about the long run. If you play a very large number of turns, your average gain per turn settles around

expected points per turn = probability of your condition × points you win

The game is fair between the two sides exactly when those two products are equal — the same expected points per turn for both. It is not about who has the bigger single win: winning 6 points with probability 1/6 has the same long-run value as winning 1 point with probability 1.

Step 3 · Simulate it and check the arithmetic

A simulation does not decide whether a game is fair — the products above do. What the simulation shows is how much a short series can wander away from that long-run value, and how the wander shrinks as the series gets longer.

How to check any claim about chance

You have just used the same four moves on three different machines. They work on any claim you will ever meet:

  1. Name the machine and its probabilities. What are the possible outcomes, and what chance does each have? If you cannot state that, you cannot reason about it yet.
  2. Predict before you look. Writing it down first is what stops you from quietly moving the goalposts afterwards.
  3. Collect real data and compare. Small samples wobble a lot. A run of 10 tells you almost nothing; 1,000 tells you much more.
  4. Explain the gap with arithmetic, not with a story. “The coin owes me” is a story. “6 of 36 pairs make a 7” is arithmetic.
Ways to describe chance honestly (the language detectives use)
  • “It is possible but unlikely” — describes a low-probability outcome; it does not say it cannot happen.
  • “About 1 in 6” — describes a long-run proportion, not a promise about the next six rolls.
  • “The last five were red, so black is due” — this is the gambler’s fallacy. An independent machine does not balance its own history. What balances a proportion is the growing total, not a debt.
  • “My sample was small” — a real and complete explanation for a surprising result, not an excuse.
Three traps this page is designed to spring (harmlessly, on purpose)
  • Expecting balance in a small sample. Ten flips can give eight heads. Nothing is broken.
  • Assuming equal outcomes mean equal totals. Two fair dice, but the sums are not equally likely, because bigger sums can be made in more ways.
  • Comparing the biggest reward instead of the expected points. A rare five-point outcome can be worth less per turn than a common one-point outcome. Multiply the probability by the points and compare the products.
