A 10-15 minute session

Case: Sleep and Grades. Who should you believe?

A headline flashes in your feed: Scientists proved: the less you sleep, the better you learn. Under it sits a chart that looks convincing. Your job is to check whether the headline survives contact with the data.

You get access to the same dataset the headline writer used: 240 records of fictional students from three schools - how much they sleep, how much time they spend on screens in the evening and how they did on a test. There are no hidden files and no right answer at the end: you have to build the conclusion yourself and defend it.

iWhat these data are

The dataset is fully synthetic. The students, schools and results were invented by the authors of this app; the numerical patterns were built into the data on purpose, so that there is something to investigate. This is not a study of real people and it says nothing about real schoolchildren. See the section Where the data come from below and the file README.md.

What you will be able to explain afterwards

  • how chart framing (in particular where the axis starts) changes the impression you get from data without changing the numbers themselves;
  • how a convenient sample differs from a random one and why a large size does not cure bias;
  • why a visible link between two quantities still does not prove that one causes the other;
  • how to build a careful, honest conclusion from observations.

How the lesson works

Three cases, each with a demonstration, your own attempt and a conclusion. The rules are simple:

  • you may try an answer as many times as you like; attempts are not counted;
  • a hint is available in every case - open it if you get stuck;
  • the notebook at the top collects your observations into one report;
  • if something goes wrong, the Reset everything button gives you a clean start.

Helpful (but not required)

  • percentages and averages;
  • reading bar charts and scatter plots;
  • a willingness to argue with a statement, not with a person.

No statistics background is needed: every term is explained along the way. The tool does the arithmetic for you - your job is to explain.

Where the data come from, and the data dictionary

To investigate data you need to know where they come from. That is described honestly here, including what the dataset does not contain.

How the dataset was built

  1. Three fictional schools are defined, differing in their school start time: 8:00 (Early Light), 10:00 (Midday), 12:00 (Evening College).
  2. Each school is given averages: sleep (h), evening screen time (h/day) and test score (out of 100). The later the lessons start, the less sleep, the more screen time and the higher the average score.
  3. A small individual variation (the evening load of each student) and random noise from a deterministic generator are added within a school. As a result the link inside a single school is weak and barely visible.
  4. 240 records in total: school A - 96, school B - 84, school C - 60. The records are stored in school blocks, not shuffled together.
  5. The same generator seed always produces the same dataset, so the results of the lesson are reproducible.
!What is not here

There are no real students, schools, countries or organisations. There are no medical, political or otherwise sensitive claims. There are no personal data: the dataset holds only an id, a school and three numbers. Any resemblance of the school names to real ones is accidental; all names are invented.

What does your sample show?

Hint for case 2

Look not at the average in the sample but at its composition. If the sample is 100% one school, then any average describes only that school, whether you drew 10 records or 200. Compare the share of each school in the sample with its share in the full dataset: the gap is the bias. Then try drawing a sample of 200 the convenient way: does it get any closer to the full dataset?

Final · a fresh application

Your own headline, in three parts

Now you write the headline yourself, but not the way the author at the start did. A good headline about data has three parts, and all three are required:

  1. a claim - what exactly is linked or changing (without the word proven);
  2. an observation - a concrete number from the data with its unit;
  3. a limitation - a caveat: under what conditions it holds and what else could explain the link.

Use at least two of the issues you worked on: chart framing, sampling bias, and the difference between association and cause.

For example, following this pattern: [what changes] is linked to [what], but [limitation]: the data show [number].

Hint for the final headline

Start with the limitation: that makes it harder to go wrong. Then add the number that supports it, for instance a difference in averages, a share or the size of the link. Only then write the conclusion itself. If you still feel like writing proven, replace it with linked or the data show.

What a strong headline looks like (a worked example)

Example: Students of three fictional schools slept less the later their lessons started, and their scores were higher, but the link almost vanishes within a single school: across all 240 records r is about -0.33, while within school C it is about -0.06.

  • Claim: slept less the later their lessons started, and their scores were higher - the link is called a link.
  • Observation: 240 records, r about -0.33, within school C about -0.06 - numbers with their context.
  • Limitation: but the link almost vanishes within a single school - it says straight out that the overall picture may be explained by differences between schools.

This is one of several possible strong headlines, not a template to copy. A headline with a different limitation (about sampling bias, say) can be just as valid, as long as it rests on the same data.
